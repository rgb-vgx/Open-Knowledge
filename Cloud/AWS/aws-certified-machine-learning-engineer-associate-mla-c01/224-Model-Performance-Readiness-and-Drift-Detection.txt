So, let's talk about drift detection in machine learning, and this is measuring a couple of
different things.
There could be drift in the data that's going into your model, and there could be drift
in how your model is behaving over time as well.
In either case, if things start behaving unexpectedly, there might be some baseline that you're comparing
things against, and if you diverge too far from that baseline, it might be time to go
back and reevaluate if you need a new model.
So the idea is that you have some sort of a baseline that's capturing the expected or
reference behavior of your machine learning model, and then you compare production behavior
in the real world, what data is really coming in for inference, and what is my model really
putting out there, and is this diverging in time from what I expected back when I originally
trained this model.
So I can measure that on the data side, for example, by having feature distributions or
ranges or missing value rates, categorical proportions, mean, standard deviations.
The idea here is that you're measuring the characteristics of the data that you used
to train this model, and then comparing that to the characteristics of the data that's
actually being fed in for inference that it's trying to make predictions from.
And if your data that you're seeing coming in from the real world diverges too much from
what you used for training the model, that could be nature's way of telling you that
the world has changed, and maybe your model needs to change as well.
On the performance side, if you have ground truth results on what your predictions are
and how they differ from reality, you can use that to measure things like accuracy and
precision and recall and F1 score and all these other things, AUC, RMSE, MAE, whatever
it is that's appropriate for your workload.
If you do have ground truth to compare the actual performance of your model over time,
you might have a point at which you say, it's drifted too far, I need to go back and redo things.
So data on the data side, we define drift as the distribution of production inputs shifting
away from the reference or training data.
So we have a model that is consuming new data for inference, and if that data that's coming
in starts to diverge too much from the statistical qualities of the data that you used to train
the model, that might tell you that you need to go back and retrain that model.
Then there's a model quality drift.
How the actual model itself is performing is the quality of my predictions degrading
over time, and that's going to require real world labels or ground truth or human feedback
that might come later, but you got to have that to measure how good your model is really
doing and see if it's doing all right over time.
Now if your data is drifting, that doesn't automatically mean you have a problem, but
it does mean that your model is seeing data that it wasn't trained on.
Maybe your model generalizes and that's fine, but it might not.
So it's just worth a look if you see changing trends in your data that's coming into your
system versus what you trained it on.
So when you're seeing data drift, that's telling you that your input distribution changed.
Your reference is what you trained the model with, but what you're seeing in production is differing.
And then model quality drift is just saying, well, when I first deployed this model and
I measured its performance, it was at this baseline, but over time, maybe it's getting worse.
And there might be a point at which I say, I got to go back and retrain this thing or
use a different model.
How do I detect this?
So again, when you're doing your training, you have a set of reference data that you
use for training your model.
That's going to establish your baseline of what things look like on the data side.
And then on the inference side, you have inference data coming in from production, from the real world, right?
And you can compute statistics on that inference data and compare that to your baseline training data.
So over time you accumulate samples of the data that's being fed into your system from
the real world and compare its distribution to the data that was used to train your model.
If that comparison to the baseline differs using statistical tests and metrics and thresholds
that you define, you might have to go back and alert the team.
You probably don't want to automatically trigger something at that point because going back
and rebuilding that model and retraining that model is going to be a very large and
expensive task, right?
So you want a human in the loop there to make sure they make that judgment call as to whether
that's really warranted or not.
But after inspecting the upstream data and potentially retraining and re-evaluating the
